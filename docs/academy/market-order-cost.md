# Why a market order costs more than the price you saw

The price beside a coin is a useful reference, but it is not a promise about your next fill. It might be the last trade, a midpoint, or a price from another venue. A market order asks for execution now against the liquidity that is available. It does not ask everyone to sell to you at the headline price.

Imagine an order book whose lowest sell offer is one coin at $100. The next seller offers two coins at $101. If you buy three coins immediately, you pay $100 for the first and $202 for the next two. Your total is $302, and your average fill is $100.6667 before fees. The visible best offer was $100, but it applied to only one coin. Buying more walked the book. Selling works in the opposite direction: a large market sell consumes successive bids, usually at lower prices.

Spread is another cost. The best bid is what someone will pay; the best ask is what someone will accept. Crossing from one side to the other costs you the gap. A market buy normally takes the ask, and a market sell normally takes the bid. Neither is guaranteed to match the last trade. Trading fees then add a separate charge. A fill receipt should let you distinguish execution price, quantity and fees rather than roll every difference into one mysterious number.

A decentralised constant-product pool expresses the same scarcity differently. Instead of discrete offers, its reserves follow an invariant. Your purchase removes the asset you buy and adds the asset you spend, moving the reserve ratio. A larger trade therefore receives a worse average price even if nobody else trades while you are watching. That predictable effect of your own size is price impact. Slippage is the difference between the execution you expected and the execution you actually receive; other orders arriving first can contribute to it.

Try the existing [Price impact calculator](/tools/price-impact-calculator/). Set token A reserve to 100, token B reserve to 300000, spending to 15000, and pool fee to 0%. The starting price is 3000 B per A. The pool gives you 4.761905 A, so your average execution price is 3150 B per A: 5% above the starting price. This is a reproducible arithmetic example, not a live quote. The ending marginal price is different again; it describes the next tiny trade, not the average of the order you just placed.

Now increase spending to 30000 with the same reserves and zero fee. You receive 9.090909 A at an average price of 3300: 10% above the starting price. Finally return spending to 15000 and set the fee to 0.3%. Notice how the fee and impact both affect the average. Change only one input at a time so you can see which cost moves.

A limit order sets a price boundary, but execution may be partial or may never happen. A slippage tolerance is also a boundary, not a discount: it does not create liquidity or guarantee that you will pay the headline price. Before a paper trade, compare size with available depth, inspect the estimated average fill, and include fees. After it, compare the actual receipt with that estimate. The lesson is to understand the execution you requested and the evidence you received.
